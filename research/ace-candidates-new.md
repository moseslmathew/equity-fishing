## Aeroflex Industries — a real AI infrastructure revenue stream, with order visibility limits

Aeroflex merits inclusion because it already earns money from secondary fluid network cooling skids. Its Q1 FY27 presentation reports ₹32.4 crore from this business, against consolidated operating revenue of ₹145.38 crore; consolidated PAT was ₹18.79 crore. This is materially stronger evidence than describing a hose manufacturer as an AI beneficiary solely because data-centre investment is rising. The technical problem is practical: dense computing racks need dependable fluid distribution, leak control and installation-specific assemblies. Execution, traceability and approved designs can create customer stickiness; they do not establish an unassailable technology monopoly. [A1]

Managing director Asad Daud told the July call that capacity expansion follows customer discussions, but the same exchange reveals only roughly two months of tentative dispatch schedules. Design approvals can postpone production, and the end data-centre owner is generally the customer's customer. Thus capacity and customer projections must not be treated as a firm multiyear order book. The thesis improves when commissioned capacity translates into repeat shipments, cash collection and broader customers. [A2]

The October 12 rally anticipates an October 14 board meeting; expansion was still pending as of this report. The price reaction demonstrates enthusiasm, not proof that new returns justify the purchase price. [A3] Vertiv's October 12 report of an Indian 30-MW liquid-cooling deployment independently supports the industry's direction, but does not identify Aeroflex as its supplier. [A4] The balanced stance is to include Aeroflex as a high-potential, high-expectation case and insist on sustained unit economics rather than extrapolating one strong quarter.

## Shaily Engineering Plastics — device engineering gives exposure to therapeutic innovation

Shaily's most interesting change is a shift from generic moulding economics towards drug-delivery devices. In its August call, management reported healthcare revenue of about ₹142 crore, 51% of consolidated revenue, and approximately nine million devices shipped during the June quarter. This establishes an operating business, not merely a bet on the global obesity market. Precision, reproducible dosage delivery, tooling, validation and integration with pharmaceutical partners are the relevant quality questions. The evidence does not permit a claim that end patients universally prefer its pens or that competing devices cannot displace them. [S1]

Amit Sanghvi's team explicitly discusses supplier-line efficiency, customer supply-chain issues and partner dependence. Those admissions matter more than a broad addressable-market slide. The call expected additional capacity of 25 million pens by the end of September, taking installed capacity to approximately 75 million; this is guidance from August, not independent confirmation that commissioning occurred. A third-party September article gives different capacity figures, so the primary transcript should control. [S1,S2]

Public investors are attracted to a higher-value healthcare mix, but call participants also ask about price competition, margins and partner launch timing. These are sensible objections. The consumer division's weakness means group growth can remain less spectacular than the device story. [S2] Recent Pharmarack reporting shows GLP-1 sales-value growth alongside lower units: value headlines alone cannot predict pen shipments. [S3] The investment case strengthens with actual partner launches, rising utilisation, repeat orders and returns on incremental capital. It weakens if new capacity arrives ahead of demand or the stock's valuation requires every programme to succeed. Prefer inclusion as a differentiated growth case, while leaving a purchase price undecided pending a verified valuation.

## Yasho Industries — specialised chemistry with encouraging execution, still capital intensive

Yasho offers an evidence-based example of research spending turning into commercial production. Its August earnings call describes a rising contribution from new chemistries, customer-linked development and improved plant utilisation. Consolidated June-quarter revenue was about ₹308 crore, EBITDA ₹74.42 crore and PAT about ₹36 crore. Management attributes this to volume, product mix and operating leverage, rather than inventory-price gains. The research case is therefore about qualification and repeat industrial demand, not assigning fashionable semiconductor or AI labels to a diversified chemicals supplier. [Y1]

Parag Jhaveri describes increasing use of formula-based long-term supply arrangements. Such contracts can improve predictability, but they remain management-described commitments, not publicly reviewed take-or-pay guarantees. Planned FY27 capital expenditure rose to ₹250 crore. Construction, customer approvals and commissioning are distinct steps, and management's FY28 revenue ambition is not an assured forecast. The recent quarter's stronger margins should be stressed under lower utilisation and less favourable mix. [Y1]

The company's reported working-capital cycle improved but remained long; planned additional borrowing keeps financing and cash conversion central to the thesis. An apparent improvement in debt/EBITDA can also come from a stronger earnings denominator, so it should not automatically be described as equivalent debt repayment. [Y1] The investor debate in the call focuses appropriately on whether unusually strong margins can persist and how much capacity customers have committed to absorb. Our assessment is cautiously positive: the business appears to be earning a better product mix, yet quality will be proven by repeat volumes, cash release and incremental returns, not one quarter's margin. Valuation should use normalised earnings and debt after expansion, rather than annualising the best quarter.

## Tanfac Industries — downstream expansion must earn its capital

Tanfac's growth case connects established fluorochemical processing to solar-grade chemicals and a planned refrigerant expansion. June-quarter revenue rose to ₹187.2 crore, but PAT fell to ₹16.8 crore from ₹19.4 crore a year earlier. These figures prevent a simplistic claim that a technology-related market automatically brings rising shareholder profits. Management reported raw-material and fuel pressure with a lag before customer-price adjustments. Product purity, process consistency and safe handling are commercially important, yet public materials reviewed here do not establish superior field performance against every competitor. [T1]

The key investment event is the planned ₹390 crore HFC-32 project, with 20,000 tonnes annual capacity and Q3 FY27 commissioning guidance. June's ₹250 crore QIP improved funding; raising equity nevertheless spreads future profits across more shares. The company needs to demonstrate completion, customer offtake, stable yields and satisfactory returns. Management's comments about the scale of the refrigerant market should not be confused with contracted company revenue. [T1]

October6 reporting of listing approval for preferential shares is a more recent capital-structure development, supported by the exchange-announcement index. It is funding evidence, not proof of operating success. [T2,T3] Public interest from well-known investors may make Tanfac worth investigating but cannot settle valuation or execution risk. The more durable thesis would be an integrated, dependable specialty supplier; the weaker version is a leveraged bet on refrigerant prices and an optimistic commissioning timetable. Include it conditionally, testing the case against slower ramp-up and narrower spreads. A completed plant which fails to earn its cost of capital would not validate the thesis, regardless of sales growth.

## Safari Industries — brand and distribution require proof of pricing power

Safari remains a useful consumer-quality counterweight to technology-heavy selections. The investment question is whether a familiar luggage brand can turn travel and discretionary spending into sustained margins, not simply whether people will travel more. Consolidated June-quarter revenue was ₹588.58 crore, about11.5% higher than a year earlier. Independent recent coverage flags margin pressure despite that growth. The combination is a warning against equating popularity with pricing power. [F1,F2]

Sudhir Jatia brings long luggage-industry experience and has led Safari since acquiring it in2012. Experience is relevant, but management quality should be judged through cash conversion, inventory discipline, capital allocation and results after competitive discounting. A long tenure is not itself a governance guarantee. [F3] The product range and retail presence provide a credible business foundation, while brand-site ratings remain seller-hosted evidence rather than an independent survey of durability, warranty service or customer trust. No broad claim that customers consider Safari superior is justified by the materials reviewed.

Our growth case is execution-led: better distribution, product mix and efficient sourcing/manufacturing can support profits if revenue growth arrives without excessive discounting or inventory build-up. Competition and raw-material movements can absorb those benefits. [F2] Unlike Aeroflex or Shaily, there is no need to force Safari into an emerging-technology theme. It earns a place only if the consumer franchise demonstrates resilient economics. The next results should be assessed for consolidated margins, cash tied in stock and channel demand; an institutional meeting does not establish fresh buying. Maintain it as a conditional quality case and avoid paying a valuation that assumes effortless margin recovery before the evidence appears.
